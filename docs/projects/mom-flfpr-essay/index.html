Raising Singapore's Female Labour Force Participation

NTU x MOM essay competition: a data-driven country similarity index and a policy proposal for Singapore.

Report & files ↓

Overview

Singapore's female labour force participation rate (FLFPR) was 61.25% in 2024, behind global leaders like Iceland at 70.3%. Policies only transfer well between similar countries, so before studying what other countries do, we used data to find the countries most like Singapore.

We then evaluated their parental leave, childcare, equal pay and four-day week policies through three economic frameworks, and designed a proposal suited to Singapore.

What I did

  1. Combined country-level data on female participation trends, female working hours, GDP per capita, purchasing power, cost of living and a broader similarity index.
  2. Cleaned and matched country names across sources with fuzzy matching, and filled missing values using the most similar countries.
  3. Weighted each metric by its correlation with female participation and scored every country's distance from Singapore, which identified Iceland, New Zealand and Vietnam as the closest comparators.
  4. Evaluated their policies through Human Capital Theory, Feminist Economics and Institutional Path Dependence.
  5. Proposed Community-Supported Workplace Childcare Co-operatives: employer-supported, government-subsidised childcare hubs with AI-driven scheduling and a 32-hour work week.

Results & conclusions

  • Comparator countries chosen with data rather than intuition
  • A concrete, Singapore-specific proposal grounded in evidence from Iceland, New Zealand, Vietnam and Quebec

Conclusion

Our comparison found a common thread: countries with higher female participation reduce the 'motherhood penalty' by making childcare affordable and work flexible. We combined Iceland's flexibility with Vietnam's employer-led model into a proposal for Singapore, Community-Supported Workplace Childcare Co-operatives, which fits the Smart Nation vision and Singapore's collectivist culture. Quebec's 7.7% rise in FLFPR after introducing universal childcare suggests the potential impact.

What I learnt

I learnt that 'which countries should we learn from?' can be answered with data rather than intuition. Building the similarity index taught me practical data wrangling: matching country names across sources, filling gaps sensibly and choosing weights that reflect what matters for the question. Writing with Clarence taught me to turn quantitative findings into an argument policymakers can follow, grounded in economic theory.
